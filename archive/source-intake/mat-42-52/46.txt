The remaining Magnesium science closes cleanly. NIST distinguishes the strong **Mg I 285.21251 nm** resonance line from Mg II; for Mg II the laboratory standard-air wavelengths are **279.55301 nm (k)** and **280.27056 nm (h)**, corresponding to the familiar astrophysical vacuum pair near **279.635/280.353 nm**. Those Mg II resonance profiles are powerful probes of chromospheres, velocity gradients and activity, so MAT should store wavelength medium and observational environment explicitly. ([NIST][1])

The stable-isotope layer should explicitly preserve the reference scale. Mg isotope work commonly reports \(\delta^{26}\mathrm{Mg}\) relative to DSM3, but CIAAW notes that DSM3 is not recommended as a universal international delta standard because of availability; isotope reference metadata therefore belong in every quantitative record. Mg isotope fractionation is especially useful for weathering, carbonate formation/dissolution and other low-temperature processes. ([University of Oregon][2])

The **²⁶Al–²⁶Mg system** is a different information channel: extinct ²⁶Al decayed into radiogenic ²⁶Mg and is one of the principal short-lived chronometers of the early Solar System; a commonly adopted ²⁶Al half-life is about **0.717 ± 0.017 Myr**. ([DOI][3])

Magnesium sacrificial anodes are also a real standardized engineering class rather than only a conceptual corrosion example: the current **ASTM B843-18(2025)** covers cast and extruded Magnesium-alloy anodes for cathodic protection. ([ANSI Webstore][4])

### Intellectual lineage, experiments, tables and graphs

Magnesium's discovery history needs multiple roles rather than one simplistic “discoverer.” Joseph Black distinguished magnesia from lime in **1755**; Humphry Davy produced a small/impure metallic form electrochemically in **1808**; Antoine-Alexandre-Brutus Bussy produced a substantial purer metallic sample in **1831**. ([Periodic Table][5]) Victor Grignard's organomagnesium chemistry earned half of the **1912 Nobel Prize in Chemistry**. ([Nobel Prize][6])

MgB₂ gives another clean lineage: Nagamatsu, Nakagawa, Muranaka, Zenitani and Akimitsu reported bulk superconductivity at **39 K** in 2001; Bud'ko and colleagues then measured a **1.0 K Boron isotope shift**, and later momentum-resolved work directly established distinct σ- and π-band superconducting gaps. ([Nature][7])
