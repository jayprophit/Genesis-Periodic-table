$$
{}^{21}Na\rightarrow{}^{21}Ne+e^++\nu_e
$$

Using neutral atomic masses:

$$
M({}^{21}Na)=20.99765469\ u
$$

$$
M({}^{21}Ne)=20.993846685\ u
$$

Electron-capture mass difference:

$$
Q_{EC}
=
[M_p-M_d]c^2
$$

}
$$

$$
Q_{\beta^+}
=
Q_{EC}-2m_ec^2
$$

\boxed{
Q_{\beta^+}\approx2.52514\ {\rm MeV}
}
$$

# FILE: records/0011-Sodium-Na/calculations/0011-Sodium-Na-CALC-002-Na22-Decay-Q.md

# CALC-002 — Sodium-22 Decay Energetics

$$
{}^{22}Na\rightarrow{}^{22}Ne
$$

Atomic masses give:

$$
\boxed{
Q_{EC}\approx2.84320\ {\rm MeV}
}
$$

Ground-state positron-emission allowance:

$$
\boxed{
Q_{\beta^+,gs}
\approx1.82120\ {\rm MeV}
}
$$

However, the dominant decay populates the approximately:

$$
1274.537\ {\rm keV}
$$

excited state of ²²Ne.

Therefore the familiar β⁺ branch has a much smaller positron endpoint scale:

$$
1.82120-1.274537
\approx
0.54666\ {\rm MeV}
$$

before small recoil corrections.

Important rule:

```text
TOTAL DECAY Q
!=
POSITRON ENDPOINT OF THE DOMINANT EXCITED-STATE BRANCH
```

# FILE: records/0011-Sodium-Na/calculations/0011-Sodium-Na-CALC-003-Na24-BetaMinus-Q.md

# CALC-003 — Sodium-24 Beta-Minus Decay

$$
{}^{24}Na
\rightarrow
{}^{24}Mg+e^-+\bar\nu_e
$$

Atomic masses:

$$
M({}^{24}Na)=23.990962950\ u
$$

$$
M({}^{24}Mg)=23.985041697\ u
$$

For β⁻ decay:

$$
Q_{\beta^-}
=
[M_p-M_d]c^2
$$

$$
\boxed{
Q_{\beta^-}\approx5.51561\ {\rm MeV}
}
$$

This total Q value must not be confused with the electron endpoint for a particular branch because much of the decay proceeds through excited ²⁴Mg levels followed by gamma emission.

For ²³Na itself, the nuclear spin is \(I=3/2\), so it is both NMR-active and quadrupolar; its Larmor frequency scales with magnetic field rather than being a fixed “Sodium frequency.” The ground-state atomic hyperfine constant is about **885.813 MHz**, while the D-line excited states introduce additional magnetic-dipole and electric-quadrupole structure. That makes Sodium a particularly useful bridge between atomic spectroscopy and condensed/biological NMR.

Liquid Sodium is similarly state-specific. Argonne's current Cardinal implementation derives density, enthalpy, heat capacity and thermal conductivity from the Fink–Leibowitz Sodium correlations rather than treating these as fixed constants.  The NaK implementation likewise treats eutectic NaK with a potassium mass fraction of **0.778**, reinforcing the legacy point that alloying can dramatically alter the accessible liquid range.

DOE's materials work also supports preserving sodium alanate as a **hydrogen-storage research material**, while keeping the distinction between material capacity and complete storage-system performance.

The battery section benefits from a similar distinction. Current 2026 literature treats hard carbon as the leading practical Sodium-ion anode, but its storage mechanism is still not reducible to one universally settled cartoon: pseudo-graphitic domains, interlayer spacing, surface sites, pores and especially **closed porosity** all contribute.  Solid electrolytes now span oxide, sulfide, halide and related families, while PBAs remain highly sensitive to crystal structure, vacancies, water and defects.

The high-pressure layer has also improved since the original record. Sodium's room-temperature sequence is approximately **bcc → fcc → cI16 → oP8 → tI19 → hP4** at about 65, 103, 118–119, 125 and 200 GPa.  More importantly, a 2025 single-crystal experiment at **223 GPa** provided experimental evidence supporting interstitial electron localization in transparent hP4 Sodium, strengthening the classification from merely “electride-like” to **experimentally supported electride state**.
